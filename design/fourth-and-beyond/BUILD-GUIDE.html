Squarespace 7.1 · Step by step

Building the Fourth & Beyond website

Written for someone who does not code and does not design. Every click is named, every value is given, and all the wording is ready to copy. Work top to bottom — each part assumes the one before it is done.

How long this takes Roughly a day for the waitlist site — Parts 0 to 4, then Part 6 — if you have your logo files and email tool ready. Parts 1 and 2 are the bulk of it. Part 5 is for later, when you open the shop; skip it for now. Nothing here is permanent — every change can be undone.

1

Set up your brand

About 45 minutes · do this once, it applies everywhere

1.1

Enter your five brand colours

Squarespace 7.1 uses a palette of exactly five colours — which is exactly what you have.

  1. Click Website in the left menu, then Site styles (the paintbrush icon).
  2. Click Colours, then Edit palette.
  3. Click each of the five swatches in turn and type in the code below. Include the #.
SlotColourCode to typeNow used for
1Soft Sky Blue#A8C4D8Main background
2Deep Green#2E3830Accent — text, buttons, labels
3Cream#FAF8F4Cards and button text
4Pale Sage#E8E8D8Banded sections
5Berry/Coral#CC7C72Logo ampersand and small rules only

Squarespace will now generate a set of themes from these — named things like “Lightest 1” and “Dark 1”. You will pick a theme for each section later. Do not worry about them yet.

1.2

Set Alice as your heading font

  1. Still in Site styles, click Fonts.
  2. Click Assign styles, then Headings.
  3. In the font list, search for Alice and select it.
Important: never make a heading boldAlice only comes in one weight. If you bold a heading, the browser fakes it by smearing the letters and it looks visibly wrong. The Custom CSS in step 1.4 prevents this automatically — but do not manually bold headings either.
1.3

Set your body font

Calibri is not free for websites — having it in Microsoft Office does not cover web use. You have two choices:

  1. Try Carlito first (free). It is a copy of Calibri with identical letter widths, so it looks near-identical and costs nothing. Search the font list for Carlito. If it is there, choose it and you are done.
  2. Or buy the Calibri web licence. If Carlito is not available or you want the real thing, buy a webfont licence (search “Calibri webfont licence” — it is sold by Monotype). You will be given .woff2 files. Then in Fonts, use the option to upload a custom font and select those files.

Then click Assign styles → Paragraphs and choose whichever you set up.

1.4

Paste in the Custom CSS

This is the only code in the whole guide. You do not need to understand it — copy it whole and paste it in. If anything looks wrong afterwards, delete it and everything returns to normal.

  1. Go to Website → Website tools → Custom CSS.
  2. Click into the empty box and paste everything below.
  3. Click Save.
/* ============================================
   FOURTH & BEYOND - brand styling
   ============================================ */

/* Headings never bold - Alice has only one weight */
h1, h2, h3, h4 {
  font-weight: 400 !important;
  letter-spacing: -0.015em;
}

/* Turn "Heading 4" into the small label above headlines */
h4 {
  text-transform: uppercase !important;
  letter-spacing: 0.16em !important;
  font-size: 0.75rem !important;
  color: #2E3830 !important;
}

/* Buttons: deep green, coral on hover */
.sqs-block-button-element,
.sqs-button-element--primary {
  background-color: #2E3830 !important;
  color: #FAF8F4 !important;
  border-radius: 2px !important;
  transition: background-color 0.18s ease;
}
.sqs-block-button-element:hover,
.sqs-button-element--primary:hover {
  background-color: #1C231E !important;
}

/* Blog category tags: cream chip, green text */
.blog-categories a,
.blog-meta-item--categories a {
  background: #FAF8F4;
  color: #2E3830 !important;
  padding: 0.2em 0.6em;
  border-radius: 2px;
  text-transform: uppercase;
  letter-spacing: 0.12em;
  font-size: 0.7rem;
  text-decoration: none !important;
}
What that didIt stopped headings going bold, turned the “Heading 4” text style into your small coral label, made buttons deep green with a coral hover, and styled your blog category tags as sky-blue chips.
On the colours in that codeThe page background is now Soft Sky Blue and Deep Green does the work of text, buttons and labels. Two consequences to keep: coral can no longer be used for text — it is unreadable on blue at any size, so it survives only in the logo ampersand and a few thin rules; and category tags are now cream chips with green text, because sky blue cannot sit on itself. Please keep both as they are.

The two buttons. These matter more than anything else on the page.

  1. Beneath the paragraph, add a Button block labelled Gifting for someone else.
  2. Add a second beside it labelled For myself. Drag until level.
  3. First = solid style, second = outline style.
  4. Point both at the waitlist form at the bottom of the page.
Never write “Buy” or “Shop” on these buttonsNothing is on sale until spring 2027. A “Buy” button that lands on a waitlist form reads as bait and switch, and it is the fastest way to lose someone’s trust on their first visit. The current labels still work at launch, when you relink them to the shop.
Why both buttons go to the same placeThey are not two funnels. They exist so a gift-buyer sees themselves on the page within three seconds. “Gifting for someone else” goes on the left — it is read first and it is the majority case.
2.3

Mothers know best

Add section → single column of text. Background: lightest blue.

Heading 2

Mothers know best

Paragraph

We asked over 130 mothers what they actually needed after giving birth.

They told us what they wished someone had given them.

Heading 3 — on its own line

So we built the thing they described.

How this section is builtThree beats: what you asked, what they said, what you did about it. The middle line reports what mothers told you — which your own survey evidences directly — rather than claiming what they did or did not receive, which you would have to prove separately.
Leave the statistics out for nowThe three figures — 78%, 40%, one in five — are on hold until you have a citable sample size and date. Build the section without them. The copy above reads perfectly well as it stands, so there is no gap to fill.

When the figures clear, they go between “Almost none of them had been given it” and “So we built the thing they described”, with a line naming the sample and the date it was collected.

2.8

The fourth trimester

Add section → list or accordion, five rows. Label: The fourth trimester. Headline: Twelve weeks. And what happens after them.

Row titleText underneath
Days 1–3
Getting home		Comfort and practical recovery essentials for the days when leaving the house is not an option.
Days 4–14
Healing and rest		Support for physical recovery, with guidance on what is normal and the signs worth calling someone about.
Days 15–42
Finding a rhythm		Feeding, sleep and mental health — up to the six-week check, which most people are told is the finish line.
Days 43–84
Where most support stops		It isn’t the finish line. The fourth trimester runs a further six weeks past that appointment, and this is the stretch nobody prepares you for. Some of the care you are entitled to only arrives if you know to ask for it — and nobody tells you what to ask.
Day 85 onwards
And beyond		Longer-term recovery, pelvic health and returning to yourself. The fourth trimester ends; the recovery doesn’t — which is the half we are named after.
Get these two numbers the right way roundThe fourth trimester is twelve weeks — 84 days. The 42-day mark is the six-week check, which is where NHS support tails off, not where the fourth trimester ends. Conflating them is the single easiest mistake to make on this site, and it undercuts your own argument: the gap you exist to fill is precisely the six weeks between those two numbers.
Say “some of the care” — do not put a number on itIt is tempting to write “you only get two health visitor checks in the first year”. Don’t, not on the live site. Health visiting is commissioned locally and provision varies considerably between areas, so a single national figure is very likely to be wrong for a good share of your readers — and being wrong about someone’s NHS entitlement is a worse error than being vague.

The qualitative version is the persuasive part anyway, and it is true everywhere. Save the specifics for a resource hub article where you can cite the guidance and note that it varies.
Keep the last two rowsDays 43–84 is the gap the business is built on. Day 85 onwards is the only place your name gets explained — the fourth trimester, and then beyond it.

Build the resource hub

About 45 minutes

3.1

Create the blog

  1. In Pages, click + and choose Blog.
  2. Name it Resource Hub.
  3. In its settings, set the URL to /resource-hub.
3.2

Set up your five categories

Categories are how readers filter the hub. Create these five exactly, and put every post in one of them:

  • Recovery
  • Mental health
  • Feeding
  • Pelvic health
  • For partners
  • Knowing what to ask
On that last categoryIt covers what postnatal care exists, what is offered automatically and what you have to request. It is the highest-value thing you can publish: it is what people actually search for, it is the sort of page that gets bookmarked and forwarded, and being the clearest source on it wins you the topic.

Avoid calling it “Know your rights”. Much NHS postnatal provision is guidance and local commissioning, not an enforceable right, and promising rights you cannot substantiate is a credibility risk on a health-adjacent site. The category label stays warm; put the search language in the article titles instead — that is where it earns its keep.

You add a category when writing a post, in the post’s settings panel. Once posts exist, the category links appear automatically and the CSS from step 1.4 styles them as sky-blue chips.

3.3

Add a signup prompt to every post

At the bottom of each post, add the same signup form with this wording:

Get the next one first.

Join the waitlist for early access to our kits, journals and service vouchers — and new resources as we publish them.

Do not publish the sample articlesThe six article titles in the mockup are invented placeholders, written so the design could be judged. None has been clinically reviewed. Every real article needs sign-off from Irati or Evone before it goes live.
4

Connect the waitlist form

About 30 minutes

This is the single most important thing on the site to get right. A form that silently fails loses you signups you will never know about.

4.1

If you chose Squarespace Forms

  1. In a section, click Add block → Form.
  2. Delete every field except Email.
  3. Set the button text to Join the waitlist.
  4. In the form’s Storage settings, connect it to your email address and, if you like, a Google Sheet.
4.2

If you chose Beehiiv or ConvertKit

  1. In that tool, create a signup form and choose the embed option. Copy the code it gives you.
  2. In Squarespace, click Add block → Code, and paste it in.
  3. Set up a welcome email that sends automatically on signup. This is the point of choosing this option — do not skip it.
4.3

Test it properly

  1. Open your live site in a private/incognito window.
  2. Sign up with a real email address you can check.
  3. Confirm the email actually arrives, and that the address appears in your list.
  4. Repeat on your phone.
Do this before you tell anyone the site is liveTest every form on the site — hero, closing section, and blog posts. They are separate blocks and any one of them can be misconfigured on its own.
5

Gifting and registries

Do this when you open the shop — not needed for the waitlist site

Most of your customers would rather be given a kit than buy one. Baby registries and gift lists are where those decisions get made, so this part makes sure you can appear on them.

The good news: you do not need a deal with anyone. Gift lists like MyRegistry, Giftwhale, GiftList and Prezola let a parent add any product by pasting its web address. Your only job is to make your pages readable to them.

5.1

Make your product pages registry-ready

When a parent pastes your product link into a gift list, that site reads your page and pulls out the name, price and picture. For that to work, each product needs:

  • Its own web address — a real product page, not a pop-up or several products sharing one page.
  • One clear main image.
  • A visible price on the page.
  • No login or password in front of it.

Squarespace product pages do all of this correctly by default. You mainly need to avoid breaking it — so give each of your three products its own product page rather than describing all three on one.

5.2

Add the registry prompt — the important one

This is the highest-value thing in this part, and it is just a line of text.

Gift lists do not make people aware of you — they only capture people who already know you. So rather than hoping a gift-giver stumbles across you, prompt the mum to put you on the list her friends are already shopping from.

On each product page, add a text block underneath the Add to Cart button:

Small text under the buy button

Having a baby shower? Add this to your baby registry — most gift lists let you add any item by pasting this page’s link.

Why this worksIt turns your gifting strategy around. Instead of a stranger having to find you, the person who wants the kit puts it in front of the people already planning to buy her something. It costs you one text block.
5.3

Sell a gift card

This solves the biggest gifting obstacle: someone wants to buy for her but does not know her due date, her size, or what she already has.

  1. Go to Selling → Products and click Add product.
  2. Choose Gift Card as the product type.
  3. Set a few fixed amounts — £25, £50, £100 — and allow a custom amount if you want.
  4. Choose whether the code is emailed straight to the recipient or sent to the buyer as a printable PDF to hand over. For a gifting-led brand, offer both.
  5. Link it from your Gifting section: Not sure what she needs? Send a gift card.
How it works for the buyerThey buy it like any product. The recipient gets a unique code and enters it at checkout. It can be spent on physical products, downloads and services.
Two limits to knowGift cards cannot be used for subscriptions or subscription pricing plans — so if you ever sell a recurring kit, cards will not cover it. And they need a commerce-capable plan; check yours before you advertise them. If your plan lacks them, the stand-in is a normal product called “Gift voucher” that you fulfil by emailing a code by hand.
5.4

Apply to MyRegistry’s retailer programme

Free, and it puts your brand and logo inside a network used by a large number of registry creators.

  1. Go to MyRegistry’s retailer/business pages and apply as a merchant.
  2. Tell them you are on Squarespace, not Shopify, and ask exactly what they can offer you.
Set your expectations hereTheir easy self-install button is built for Shopify. On Squarespace you may only get a directory listing rather than an “Add to Registry” button on your own pages. Still worth applying — just ask before you spend time on it.
5.5

Two things to skip, and two to check

ItemVerdict
BabylistSkip for now. The biggest universal registry, but US-focused. Revisit only if you ship to the US.
Building your own wishlist featureSkip. You have three products, not forty — there is nothing to curate. A gift card and the 5.2 prompt cover the same ground.

6

Before you launch

About 30 minutes

Work through this list. The first four are the ones that actually cost you if missed.

  • Every form tested on desktop and phone, with a real email that arrived.
  • Nothing published that has not been clinically checked. No placeholder articles, no health claims without sign-off.
  • No statistics on the page unless the sample size and collection date are stated alongside them.
  • “Built with clinicians” is not in the footer unless advisory sign-off has come through.
  • Medical disclaimer in the footer: “The information here is for general guidance and does not replace advice from your midwife, GP or health visitor.”
  • Checked on a phone. Most of your visitors will be on one. Scroll the whole site.
  • No heading is bold anywhere.
  • Advisory board section is hidden — not live with empty frames or draft names.
  • Logo readable on every background, including the sage and green sections.
  • Privacy policy page exists and is linked in the footer — you are collecting email addresses, so under UK GDPR you need one.
  • Page title and description set for both pages, under each page’s SEO settings.
  • Favicon uploaded (Design → Browser icon).
  • When the shop opens: each product on its own public page with a clear image and visible price, so gift lists can read it.
